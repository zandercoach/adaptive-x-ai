IMAGE STYLE GUIDE ("Zander Flipchart" — use for every future post)
====================================================================

Hand-drawn flipchart illustration, as sketched live by a trainer with markers: clean black marker outlines on a plain white background, simple friendly shapes, hand-lettered text. Human figures are always drawn with a body — a round head, an oval torso, and single-line arms and legs attached to that torso, each ending in a small round hand or foot, never a head with limbs hanging directly off it — and the posture carries the expression. This applies to the human figures only; the robot of this series keeps his own look (boxy head with antenna, segmented arms and legs) and is not changed by it. Accents only in classic flipchart marker colors — yellow, orange, green, blue, red — applied as wide, slightly uneven marker strokes. No gradients, no photorealism, no dark shading, plenty of white space. Light, warm, optimistic mood. Square composition. Captions are hand-lettered in black marker and underlined in black as well; the underline belongs to the lettering and is not a colour accent.


IMAGE PROMPT ("Dreimal ist noch keine Regel") — GOAL, NOT A CHECKLIST
==============================================================

This brief tells you what the picture is for and leaves the motif to you. It does not describe the scene element by element.

WHAT THE POST SAYS
Three posts in a row had ended by announcing what the next one would be about. On the fourth, the pairing agent proposed writing that into the voice guide as binding. Christian said no: the teaser had never been agreed, it had simply fitted three times. The same evening a one-off decision about how the team structure gets published stood in the post plan as a rule, and was struck out again. Conventions come out of practice: they are discussed together and then put into force. Observing a repetition does not produce one. A pattern you have seen three times is an observation; it becomes a rule the moment a team has talked about it and wants to be bound by it. Otherwise an observation quietly turns into a requirement for everybody, only because somebody noticed it. With an agent this is visible, because he writes his proposal down and submits it; among people the same thing happens invisibly, and "that is how we do it here" was often never decided by anyone. The close is honest rather than principled: the rule was already written in Christian's head before he said no.

WHAT THE PICTURE SHOULD DO
Someone scrolling past should feel the gap between "it has happened three times" and "we agreed on it", and should read the refusal as friendly rather than as a reprimand. The mood is collegial and light, two colleagues working out how a rule comes about, not a correction. Steer away from: legal and courtroom imagery (gavels, paragraph signs, law books), a raised index finger, stop signs and red crossings-out as scolding, ticked checklists as the whole idea, and any composition where one figure stands above or in front of the other.

WHAT IS FIXED
The style guide above. Square composition, white background. Both figures of this series carry the picture: the stick-figure person and the small friendly stick-figure robot, exactly these two and no invented third, because the story is a proposal and an answer and needs two. They face each other as equals. Any hand-lettering in the picture is German, since the post is German. A hand-lettered caption at the bottom in black marker, underlined in black, reading exactly: "Dreimal ist noch keine Regel". Apart from the caption the picture carries no words. As always, fetch two existing post PNGs from the same folder as visual reference so the series keeps one look.

WHAT IS YOURS
The motif, the composition, and which single detail carries the message. Pick one clear idea and let it breathe rather than assembling several. How the picture shows the difference between three occurrences and one agreement is yours to decide.
