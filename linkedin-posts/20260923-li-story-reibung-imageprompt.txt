IMAGE STYLE GUIDE ("Zander Flipchart" — use for every future post)
====================================================================

Hand-drawn flipchart illustration, as sketched live by a trainer with markers: clean black marker outlines on a plain white background, simple friendly shapes, hand-lettered text. Human figures are always drawn with a body — a round head, an oval torso, and single-line arms and legs attached to that torso, each ending in a small round hand or foot, never a head with limbs hanging directly off it — and the posture carries the expression. This applies to the human figures only; the robot of this series keeps his own look (boxy head with antenna, segmented arms and legs) and is not changed by it. Accents only in classic flipchart marker colors — yellow, orange, green, blue, red — applied as wide, slightly uneven marker strokes. No gradients, no photorealism, no dark shading, plenty of white space. Light, warm, optimistic mood. Square composition. Captions are hand-lettered in black marker and underlined in black as well; the underline belongs to the lettering and is not a colour accent.


IMAGE PROMPT ("Reibung") — GOAL, NOT A CHECKLIST
==============================================================

This brief tells you what the picture is for. The motif is named below rather than
left open, which is a departure from the usual form: the first version of this
image worked from an open brief and could not be read (review of 18.09, "I do not
understand the picture — friction, effortlessness?"). The staging is still yours.

WHAT THE POST SAYS
Christian changed the design direction of his brand twice this summer, and both
times it was done quickly, because a revision with an agent costs nothing. Had a
person worked on it, somebody would have asked why, pushed back, and put a third
proposal on the table that neither of them had brought into the room. That is what
falls away: a revision that costs nothing costs nobody anything, and what costs
nothing brings nothing either. He now notices it in himself. He formulates less
than he did in June and reviews more of what his two agents produce, and the better
that gets, the more lightly he reviews. The risk is not that the output gets worse,
it is that his own attention gets cheaper without him noticing, and "then I will
review harder" is an answer he does not believe from himself. What he does instead
is fetch feedback on strategy and design from people who know the subject, for two
reasons in this order: their expert judgement, which he trusts over his agent's on
design and innovation, and the disagreement that comes with it, because the
uncomfortable conversation is where the third idea comes from. Friction alone is not
enough. In teams the same holds without any AI: whoever makes disagreement
expensive gets fast agreement and few ideas.

WHAT THE PICTURE SHOULD DO
Someone scrolling past should feel the effortlessness first and sense a second later
that something has gone missing with it. Ease, then loss. The loss sits on the
human's side, not the agent's: this is the post's own sharpest line, that the risk
is him. Not a warning, not a telling-off, and not a picture that makes the agent the
culprit.

THE MOTIF
A review that costs nothing. The robot holds out a finished sheet; the person walks
past and stamps it through with a rubber stamp without looking at it, gaze already
elsewhere, and a small pile of identically stamped sheets lies behind him. Both are
friendly and content — waving it through is pleasant, which is the whole problem.
Steer away from: broken or grinding cogs and gears, sparks, flames, sandpaper, rope
and tug-of-war, two figures arguing, angry or sad faces, red warning signs, screens,
dashboards, and speech bubbles full of text.

WHAT IS FIXED
The style guide above. Square composition, white background. Two figures of this
series and no invented third: the stick-figure person and the robot. The colleague
who is missing must not be drawn, not even faintly or in outline — the picture shows
what is there, and the absence has to be legible from that. Both figures friendly
and content; the loss is in the situation, not on a face. No numbers, digits or
percentages anywhere. The sheets carry no writing: apart from the caption and the
stamp mark the picture has no words in it. Any hand-lettering is German, since the
post is German. A hand-lettered caption at the bottom in black marker, underlined in
black, reading exactly: "Was nichts kostet, bringt nichts". As always, fetch two
existing post PNGs from the same folder as visual reference so the series keeps one
look.

WHAT IS YOURS
The staging: where the two stand and how much air is between them, the posture of
the person walking past, how many sheets are in the pile, and which marker colour
does the work. Keep it one clear moment with room around it rather than a busy
scene.
