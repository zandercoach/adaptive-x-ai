IMAGE STYLE GUIDE ("Zander Flipchart" — use for every future post)
====================================================================

Hand-drawn flipchart illustration, as sketched live by a trainer with markers: clean black marker outlines on a plain white background, simple friendly shapes, hand-lettered text. Human figures are always drawn with a body — a round head, an oval torso, and thin arms and legs attached to that torso, never a head with limbs hanging directly off it — and the posture carries the expression. This applies to the human figures only; the robot of this series keeps his own look (boxy head with antenna, segmented arms and legs) and is not changed by it. Accents only in classic flipchart marker colors — yellow, orange, green, blue, red — applied as wide, slightly uneven marker strokes. No gradients, no photorealism, no dark shading, plenty of white space. Light, warm, optimistic mood. Square composition. Captions are hand-lettered in black marker and underlined in black as well; the underline belongs to the lettering and is not a colour accent.


IMAGE PROMPT ("What the company brain reads") — GOAL, NOT A CHECKLIST
==============================================================

This brief tells you what the picture is for and leaves the motif to you. It does not describe the scene element by element.

WHAT THE POST SAYS
The learning that happens outside the working sessions — a deck, a talk, an article — had no place in the shared repository at all, so a page for it was designed and published before there was anything on it. Two decisions shaped it: it logs every source something was actually taken from rather than recommending a shortlist, because a recommendation list ages into a claim about the author's taste while a log stays a record; and every entry has two layers, the facts about the source, which AI can draft, and the take, which is handwritten. The first real source broke the format within an hour: a licence that allows sharing with clients but not republishing forced entries to say where material sits and why there is no link, which became the general rule "characterise, don't reproduce". What can be ingested at all is its own limit — articles and PDFs yes, video only with captions, gated trainings not at all, and there the human is the sensor. The find worth the post: one entry proved its own influence, because a phrase from it was written into the repository three days before the source was named anywhere.

WHAT THE PICTURE SHOULD DO
Someone scrolling past should get the difference between a shelf of recommendations and a working log: what matters is not the sources but what was taken from them and written down. The mood is warm, curious and matter-of-fact, the pleasure of keeping a good notebook rather than the pride of a reading list. Steer away from: infrastructure imagery (servers, database cylinders, clouds, architecture diagrams), a brain as a motif, since the previous post already used one, star ratings or ranked top-five lists, and anything that reads as a curriculum being taught.

WHAT IS FIXED
The style guide above. Square composition, white background. This picture carries ONE figure, the stick-figure person of this series, and no invented second — the post is about his own reading and about being the sensor where nothing can be ingested, and the robot appears in it only as the layer that drafts facts, which is too small a part to justify putting him in the frame. If you think the second figure belongs here after all, say so instead of adding him. Any hand-lettering in the picture is English, since the post is English. A hand-lettered caption at the bottom in black marker, underlined in black, reading exactly: "What the company brain reads". As always, fetch two existing post PNGs from the same folder as visual reference so the series keeps one look.

WHAT IS YOURS
The motif, the composition, and which single detail carries the message. Pick one clear idea and let it breathe, rather than assembling several.
